    About the simulation

    What is real here

    Where the systems are. Alpha Centauri, Sirius, and TRAPPIST-1 all lie within 41 light-years of the Sun, so at the scale of the Galaxy they start in the same spot. Each one’s path is integrated from its measured position and velocity in a model Galaxy with a flat rotation curve. Press play and they drift apart, because every star follows its own orbit. The paths show where today’s motion leads. They are not a forecast.

    Time. One simulation second spans about 1.36 million years of galactic motion. The clock starts at the year 2000, and the trails show where each system came from before then.

    Close-ups. Planets and companion stars move on ellipses in their measured orbital planes, starting from their positions in 2000. Their sizes and orbits are enlarged enormously and their motion is sped up so that you can see it. The Solar System’s spacing is compressed, and TRAPPIST-1’s orbits are drawn to scale with one another. TRAPPIST-1’s planet colors are for telling them apart.

    The Galaxy. The disk turns clockwise as seen from galactic north. The arms near the Sun follow Reid et al.’s maser measurements, and the far side is inferred. The spiral pattern turns at 28 km/s per kiloparsec and the bar at 39, so stars drift through the arms. Star colors follow temperature: blue-white young stars in the arms, golden old stars in the bar.

    The sky. The Sky atlas looks out from the Sun toward catalog positions, over ESO’s Milky Way panorama. Several telescope portraits are infrared and shown in false color.
